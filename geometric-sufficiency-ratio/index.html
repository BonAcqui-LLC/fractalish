Geometric Sufficiency Ratio

GSR measures the normalized fraction of recoverable target-relevant performance captured by a declared geometric representation relative to the fuller available record.

Metric statusWorking proposal
Target dependenceExplicit and required
Comparator ruleMust be declared and fair
Known riskModel-class confounding

Formal definition

GSR_Y(G) = (SG - S0) / (SX - S0), where SX > S0

The numerator measures the gain over baseline available from the declared geometry. The denominator measures the gain over baseline available from the fuller declared record. The ratio therefore asks what fraction of the recoverable improvement survives geometric compression.

Variable definitions

  • X: fullest available system record allowed by the benchmark protocol
  • G(X): chosen geometric representation of that record
  • Y: target property, outcome, or response
  • S0: baseline score
  • SG: best validated geometry-only score under the declared evaluation rules
  • SX: best validated full-record score under the declared evaluation rules

Worked numerical example

(0.80 - 0.75) / (0.95 - 0.75) = 0.25

In this example, 80% raw accuracy sounds strong until the denominator is respected. The geometry captured only 25% of the recoverable improvement above baseline.

Why baseline normalization matters

Baseline normalization prevents easy tasks, class imbalance, and strong priors from making a geometry look more sufficient than it is. A raw geometry-only score can be high even when almost all target-relevant improvement remains outside the geometry.

Loss-based formulation

GSR^L_Y(G) = (L0 - LG) / (L0 - LX)

Use the loss form when the benchmark is defined by a proper loss rather than a score, and state the loss explicitly.

Information-theoretic formulation

GSR^I_Y(G) = I(G(X);Y) / I(X;Y)

This is a conceptual bridge to information bottleneck work. It is useful only where mutual information can be estimated credibly and the estimator assumptions are declared.

Interventional GSR

Interventional GSR replaces passive observation with intervention-response prediction. It is relevant when observational geometry can hide causal differences that only surface after perturbation.

Temporal GSR

Temporal GSR evaluates how much target-relevant information survives when developmental or event history is compressed into final-state or trajectory geometry.

Representation-stability envelope

One geometry is rarely enough. The representation-stability envelope summarizes GSR across a family of admissible geometric representations of the same record. A wide envelope means the sufficiency claim depends too heavily on representation choice.

Geometric encoding cost

Encoding cost forces a harder question: did the geometry expose structure, or did it merely absorb timestamps, labels, provenance, and interventions until it became a renamed full record?

Minimal residue-restoring channel

A useful benchmark can ask what smallest auxiliary channel restores sufficiency above a declared threshold. That channel identifies what the geometry was missing.

Benchmark design

  • Declare the equivalence relation that defines “same geometry.”
  • Match model class or state clearly how comparator power is controlled.
  • Prevent leakage, geometry smuggling, and temporally unavailable full-record features.
  • Report the raw scores, not just the ratio.

Statistical cautions

Main cautions. Comparator unfairness, model-class confounding, target leakage, geometry smuggling, unstable score choice, and overclaiming “non-geometric” as a metaphysical category.

GSR should not be treated as a magic scalar. It is only as trustworthy as the declared task, comparator, scoring rule, and evaluation budget.

Open questions

  • Can the ratio be stabilized across monotone score transformations?
  • How should uncertainty propagate through the ratio?
  • When are cross-domain comparisons meaningful?
  • What encoding-cost functional survives adversarial review?
